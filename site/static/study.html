Is good taste real?

Yes. People who picked new music well in earlier years kept picking well years later. For most good pickers the edge is modest, but the best are remarkable: the new artists found by the top 1% were — as likely as similar artists to grow tenfold within a year.

The question

Some people seem to have a knack for finding music before everyone else, or for liking things that turn out to be good. But that impression is easy to fake. Someone who listens to a thousand new artists a year will find some future hits by volume alone. Someone who only listens to what's already popular will always look "right." And anyone can get lucky.

So the question isn't whether some people's picks did well. It's whether the same people keep picking well, year after year, once luck and those other explanations are taken out.

How we measured it

We treated listening like investing. When someone gets into an artist (plays them in at least four separate weeks), that's a bet. We note how popular the artist was at that moment, then look at what happened next and compare it with every other bet made that month on artists that were exactly as popular.

One bet: Florence + the Machine, July 2008

In July 2008, Florence had the same audience as — other artists; a year later she had outgrown all of them. One good bet proves little. Taste is whether someone's bets keep beating comparable ones.

A person's taste score is how their bets did on average compared with the bets around them. The real test is whether a score built from someone's earlier years predicts how their bets do two to three years later, on completely different music.

We ran everything twice, on two separate platforms with different people: Last.fm (— listeners, 2005–2013) and ListenBrainz (— listeners, 2005–2026). Each time, we wrote down our predictions before looking.

"Good taste" can mean different things, so we measured four kinds, each for single songs, for artists, and for whole scenes (exact definitions are in the appendix):

  • Discernment: other people who try your picks tend to stick with them.
  • Community-leading: people with taste like yours adopt your picks later.
  • Prescience: your picks grow more than comparable ones.
  • Durability: your picks are still listened to in 2026.

What we found, in short

  1. Taste is real and it lasts. Of the top 1% at picking songs, — were still in the top fifth two to three years later and only — fell to the bottom fifth, against 20% each by chance.   5. Taste still works in the algorithm era. On ListenBrainz, the gap between good and bad pickers' finds grew from — in 2010–2013 to — in 2021–2025, though the best pickers' edge at the rarest breakouts is smaller than it was in the early 2010s.
  6. It's judgment, not habits. Choosing the top 1% by listening habits finds pickers at —; by track record, —.
  7. Good taste is right scenes and right artists. For the top 1%, their scenes took off — as often, and their artists within those scenes —.
  8. A good ear carries across genres, and matters least in established ones. Rated only on their other genres, people picked a genre about — as well as when rated on the genre itself. The gap between good and poor pickers was smallest in rock, folk, and jazz, blues, and classical, where most of what people get into is long established.
  9. A good picker's obsessions are their best calls. People's most-replayed finds beat their least-replayed ones, and good pickers' edge lives almost entirely in what they got hooked on.
  10. It's a steady edge, not a nose for hits. Good taste raises the odds by about the same multiple whether the takeoff is double or a hundredfold, and judging people on hits alone doesn't work.
  11. It shows most in songs, less in artists, and least in scenes, and "others stick with your picks" is among its clearest forms.
  12. Bad taste is real too. The worst pickers' finds flop more often and take off no more often than average, and bad pickers stay bad about as reliably as good ones stay good over a few years, and longer over a decade.

The same people keep picking well

The simplest way to see it: rank everyone by how well they picked songs in earlier years, then rank them again on completely different songs two to three years later. If taste were luck, everyone would land at the middle on average. Instead, people kept much of their rank: the top 1% landed around the — percentile on average, and — of them were still in the top fifth. Much of the turnover in the middle is noise: any one snapshot of someone's taste is partly luck, so people bounce around even when their taste hasn't changed.

What makes the best pickers good

Skill, not habits: not how much they listen, how many new artists they try or commit to, how obscure or mainstream their picks are, how focused they are on a few scenes, or how many times they play something before committing. The top 1% pick about as many new artists a year as everyone else and listen about as much; their picks are a bit less popular and their listening a bit more focused, but together these habits explain little. Nor is it practice: the same people picked only slightly better after tens of thousands more plays, a gain of well under one percentage point. Choosing people by their habits alone finds pickers whose new artists took off — as often as similar artists, against — when choosing by track record.

Habits like these explain only about — of the differences in how people's later picks did, against — for their track record (both small, because single picks are so noisy).

A good ear carries across genres, and matters least in established ones

Where does taste make the biggest difference? Within each genre, we compared the new artists found by the best fifth of pickers with those found by the worst fifth, each against similar artists in the same genre. The gap was biggest in fast-moving genres like electronic, hip hop, and indie, where many of the artists people get into are newcomers, and smallest in rock and punk, folk and country, and jazz, blues, and classical. Those are established catalogs: — of the rock and — of the jazz, blues, and classical artists people got into were already around at the start of our data, against — in electronic. Picking a long-established act leaves little to foresee. Some of the bigger gaps in fast-moving genres are simply more room to be right: allowing for how much new artists' fortunes vary in each genre, the middle genres come out about even, but the established ones stay clearly lowest.

Is taste general or genre-specific? Mostly general. Take the same people and rate them two ways: on their picks in one genre, and only on their picks in every other genre. Then see how their later picks in that genre did. The best fifth on the other-genres ranking did almost as well, reaching about — of the best fifth on the genre's own ranking. Knowing a genre adds a small bonus in the fast-moving ones (a point or two in hip hop, electronic, indie, and pop) and none in the established ones. In rock, R&B, and jazz, blues, and classical, people's other genres even predicted their picks slightly better than the genre itself did: picks of long-established acts reveal little about someone's ear, so what they pick elsewhere says more (and usually gives us more picks to go on). Kept their place two to three years later

How sure we are

We hid half the listeners. All the exploring, adjusting, and fixing happened on one half. Before looking at the other half, we wrote down exactly what we would test and what would count as passing, and saved that record. Then we ran it once. Every measure passed, and for — the strength matched what the first half had shown.

We checked the takeoff and good-picker numbers the same way. They were found afterwards, on the first half. Before touching the hidden half again, we wrote down each prediction, exactly how it would be measured, and a stricter bar for passing (99% sure, since there were several), in thirteen rounds. Then we checked each once. Of thirty-two predictions, twenty-nine held.

Then we checked everything again on a second platform. ListenBrainz is a separate site with different listeners, and nothing had been analysed on it. We wrote down every prediction first, with the same bars, then ran each once. Eight of the nine main measures we could run there passed, and twenty-four of the thirty-two predictions held. The table shows every one. A few ListenBrainz charts came after that run (a decade later, careers, and one person's range); nothing was predicted for them in advance, so they're marked exploratory.

What this can't tell us

  • Takeoffs are rare. "Twice as likely" means — of new artists growing tenfold instead of —. Taste tilts the odds; it doesn't guarantee hits.
  • The takeoff and good-picker numbers include rising scenes. Being early to a scene that grows is part of what good pickers do. Compared only within the same scene, the effects are roughly half as big but still hold (one more good picker adds — growth instead of —).
  • One person's score is fuzzy. Taste scores are reliable for groups but noisy for individuals, because single picks are so unpredictable (see the chart below). It's no worse on today's listening.
  • Influence versus foresight. Someone who shares music with friends partly causes the growth they get credit for. The data has no friendships, so we can't separate the two.
  • Who these listeners are. Last.fm users who were still active in 2012–13 lean towards indie and rock, and are keener than the average listener.
  • Who ListenBrainz listeners are. People who chose to track their listening on an open-source site: a smaller, keener group than most listeners. Much of its listening before 2014 was imported from Last.fm.
  • Two weak results. Community-leading for scenes came in lower on the hidden half than the first half suggested, and durability for scenes only barely passed.

Prediction strength when bets are judged over 6, 12, or 24 months (first half). Shorter windows predict better for every measure. Part of that is simply that 6-month results are measured closer in time.

How closely people's scores on two kinds of taste go together, at the artist level (0 = unrelated, 1 = identical). Discernment and prescience go together most; durability is nearly separate.

Rating people only on whether they caught the year's breakout artists barely predicts anything (—). Rating them on all their picks does (—), and those people's later finds are the ones that take off twice as often. Hits are too rare to judge anyone by.

The four kinds of taste

Discernment
For each bet, the share of other listeners who first tried the same song, artist, or scene in the following 12 months and then stuck with it (songs: at least 4 plays over at least 3 weeks; artists: listened in 4 separate weeks within 26 weeks; scenes: kept listening in at least 3 of the 6 months starting 3 months after entering). Compared with bets on items with the same prior record of keeping new listeners.
Community-leading
For each bet, the share of the listener's taste community (listeners whose favorite scene over the previous year matches theirs) who adopted the same item in the following 12 months. Compared with bets on items equally popular within that community beforehand, and adjusted for how much that community adopted overall that month.
Prescience
For each bet, the growth in how many listeners played the item over the next 12 months (log ratio), counted within a fixed panel of listeners active at both ends, excluding the bettor. Songs use new listeners in the six months before and after. Compared with bets on items equally popular at the time.
Durability
For each bet, how many ListenBrainz users listen to the item in 2026 (log), compared with bets on items equally popular at the time.

Songs, artists, and scenes

Artists
A bet is a listener's first committed adoption of an artist: listened in at least 4 separate weeks, the 4th within 26 weeks of the first play, after the listener's first 26 weeks on Last.fm. Dated at the 4th week.
Songs
The same rule applied to single songs, for the 1/8 of listeners with week-by-week detail. Songs by one artist adopted in the same month count as one bet, so an album isn't counted many times over.
Scenes
Groups of artists that the same people listen to, rebuilt each year from earlier listening only (200 scenes). A bet is entering a scene new to the listener: their first committed artist there, with under 5% of their listening in it over the previous year.

Method details

  • Data: MLHD+ (Last.fm scrobbles, — listeners, — billion listens), split at random by listener into an explore half and a hidden (sealed) half.
  • Replication: ListenBrainz (— listeners, — billion listens, 2005–2026), artist and song names linked to MusicBrainz IDs, converted to the same layout and analysed with the same code. Predictions, bars, and the era test were written down before it was analysed; durability isn't measured there, since its outcome is ListenBrainz's own 2026 listening.
  • Every comparison is with other listeners' bets in the same month and popularity group, adjusted for scene, item age, prior growth, country, and release timing.
  • Prediction strength: rank correlation between a listener's score up to a cutoff year and their score from bets made two to three years later, after removing what impostor traits (measured up to the cutoff) explain.
  • Passing (fixed before the hidden half was analysed): Holm-corrected one-sided test within each family (discernment; the other nine) at 5%, and 3-year stability above zero.
