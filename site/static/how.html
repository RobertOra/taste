Is good taste real?

Yes. People who picked new music well in earlier years kept picking well years later. For most good pickers the edge is modest, but the best are remarkable: the new artists found by the top 1% grew tenfold within a year 4.7× as often as similar artists.

The question

Some people seem to have a knack for finding music before everyone else. But that impression is easy to fake. Someone who listens to a thousand new artists a year will find some future hits by volume alone. Someone who only listens to what's already popular will always look "right." And anyone can get lucky. So the question isn't whether some people's picks did well. It's whether the same people keep picking well, year after year, once luck and those other explanations are taken out.

How we measured it

We treated listening like investing. When someone gets into an artist (plays them in at least four separate weeks), that's a bet. We note how popular the artist was at that moment, then look at what happened next and compare it with every other bet made that month on artists that were exactly as popular. A person's taste score is how their bets did on average compared with the bets around them.

Each comparison also allows for things that aren't taste: being into a scene that was growing anyway, an artist who was already rising, a new release coming out, how new the artist was, and where they're from. The real test is whether a score built from someone's earlier years predicts how their bets do two to three years later, on completely different music.

We measured four kinds of taste, each for songs, artists and whole scenes: discernment (other people who try your picks stick with them), community-leading (people with taste like yours adopt your picks later), prescience (your picks grow more than comparable ones), and durability (your picks are still listened to today).

What we found

1. The same people keep picking well

Rank everyone by how they picked in earlier years, then again on completely different music two to three years later. If taste were luck, everyone would land in the middle. Instead, people kept much of their rank, most of all at the top.

2. The best pickers' finds take off far more often

A little for most people, a lot for a few. Out of every 1,000 new artists a middling picker found, about 2 grew tenfold within a year, the same as similar artists. For the top 1% it was about 11: 4.7× as often.

3. Their typical finds are early; their big hits last

Following each new artist for six years: a good picker's typical find pulls ahead of similar artists for a few years, then the others catch up. Bad pickers' finds keep falling behind. But the best pickers' biggest finds tend to become lasting breakouts.

4. What one new fan is worth

Turn it around and look from the artist's side. For a small artist, one new fan with a proven good track record went with 20% more growth over the next year, against 6.5% for an ordinary new fan. Once an artist is big, a good fan adds little and a poor picker counts against it. And good pickers add up: the more of them among an artist's new fans, the bigger the boost, with no sign of levelling off.

5. It's judgment, not habits

The best pickers don't listen more, try more new artists, or pick more obscure ones. Choosing the top 1% by listening habits finds pickers whose new artists took off 1.6× as often as similar artists; choosing them by track record, 4.7×. What they have is two kinds of judgment at once: they're early to scenes about to take off (2.3×), and within those scenes they pick the artists that take off (2.1×).

6. Your obsessions are your best calls

People's most-replayed finds beat their least-replayed ones, and good pickers' edge lives almost entirely in what they got hooked on.

7. Taste matters most where music moves fast

The gap between good and poor pickers was biggest in electronic, hip hop and indie, where many artists people get into are newcomers, and smallest in rock, folk and jazz, blues and classical, where most are long established. A good ear also carries across genres: rated only on their other genres, people picked a genre about two-thirds as well as when rated on the genre itself.

8. It still works in the algorithm era

Most of the Last.fm data is from before streaming took over, so we repeated everything on ListenBrainz, whose listening runs to 2026. Good pickers beat bad ones every year, and the gap grew, mostly because bad pickers' finds did worse.

9. Over a decade, good taste fades; bad taste lingers

Taste is real, but it drifts. Of the people who were top-fifth pickers in 2015, a third were still in the top fifth a year later; ten years later it was close to chance. Bad pickers stay bad longer. That's why the site scores only your last five judged years.

How sure we are

We hid half the listeners. All the exploring happened on one half of the Last.fm listeners. Before looking at the other half, we wrote down exactly what we'd test and what would count as passing. Then we ran it once: all 12 measures passed, and of 32 specific predictions, 29 held.

Then we checked it all again on ListenBrainz, a separate site with different listeners that nothing had been analysed on, with every prediction written down first: 8 of the 9 measures we could run there passed, and 24 of the 32 predictions held. A few later charts (marked exploratory) weren't predicted in advance.

We checked the obvious explanations. Every score excludes listening volume, exploring a lot, liking mainstream music, how long someone has used the service, how loyal they are to music in general, and data quality.

How the site scores you

  • Your finds: artists you played in at least four separate weeks, the fourth within six months of first hearing them. Your first six months of history are skipped, when everything looks new.
  • How each find did: how many ListenBrainz listeners first played the artist in the six months up to your find, and in the same six months a year later, compared with ListenBrainz listeners' finds of artists that were just as small that month, with the same corrections as the study (rising scenes, momentum, artist age, release timing, country).
  • Your score: how your finds did on average over your last five judged years, ranked against 14,820 ListenBrainz listeners scored exactly the same way. On ListenBrainz it agrees closely with the study's measure (0.72; about 0.95 once each score's noise is allowed for).
  • The range: where your true rank most likely sits (80%), from how reliable a score is with your number of finds. With 110 finds the score is about two-thirds signal; with 250, about four-fifths.
  • Minimums: the extended streaming history (not the one-year export), at least two years of listening, and at least 110 judged finds. Artists that fewer than two ListenBrainz listeners have played can't be judged and are left out (about 2% of finds).
  • Privacy: everything runs in your browser. It downloads small pieces of the reference data for your artists; your files never leave your computer.

Limits

  • Takeoffs are rare. "Twice as likely" means about 0.4% of new artists growing tenfold instead of 0.2%. Taste tilts the odds; it doesn't guarantee hits.
  • One person's score is fuzzy. Taste scores are reliable for groups but noisy for individuals, because single picks are so unpredictable.
  • Influence versus foresight. Someone who shares music with friends partly causes the growth they get credit for. The data has no friendships.
  • Who the listeners are. Last.fm and ListenBrainz users are keener than the average listener and lean towards indie and rock. Growth means growth among ListenBrainz listeners, not the whole world.
