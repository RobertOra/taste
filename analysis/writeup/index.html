The question

Some people seem to have a knack for finding music before everyone else, or for liking things that turn out to be good. But that impression is easy to fake. Someone who listens to a thousand new artists a year will find some future hits by volume alone. Someone who only listens to what's already popular will always look "right". And anyone can get lucky.

So the question isn't whether some people's picks did well. It's whether the same people keep picking well, year after year, once luck and those other explanations are taken out.

How we measured it

We treated listening like investing. When someone gets into an artist (plays them in at least four separate weeks), that's a bet. We note how popular the artist was at that moment, then look at what happened next and compare it with every other bet made that month on artists that were exactly as popular.

One bet: Florence + the Machine, July 2008

Share of listeners who played each artist in a given month. In July 2008, Florence had the same audience as — other artists. A year later she had grown more than any of them, while the typical one shrank slightly. Someone who got into Florence that summer made a very good bet. One good bet proves little; the question is whether a person's bets keep beating comparable ones.

A person's taste score is how their bets did on average compared with the bets around them. Then the real test: does a score built from someone's earlier years predict how their bets do two or more years later, on completely different music?

"Good taste" can mean different things, so we measured four, each for single songs, for artists, and for whole scenes:

  • Discernment: other people who try your picks tend to stick with them.
  • Community-leading: people with taste like yours adopt your picks later.
  • Prescience: your picks grow more than comparable ones.
  • Durability: your picks are still listened to in 2026.

What we found

All four kinds of taste are real to some degree. Each dot below is how well earlier taste scores predicted later results for one measure, on the hidden half of listeners. Zero would mean no prediction at all. Bars show the range we're 95% sure of.

  • Taste shows most in songs, less in artists, and least in which scenes people move into.
  • Discernment and community-leading are the strongest kinds. Spotting what others will love, and what your own crowd will pick up next, is the clearest skill.
  • "Still big in 2026" barely counts as taste for artists. Most of it is explained by simply liking popular music.
  • The effect is moderate, not huge. Good taste tilts the odds; it doesn't make someone a prophet.

How sure we are

We hid half the listeners. All the exploring, adjusting and fixing happened on one half. Before looking at the other half, we wrote down exactly what we would test and what would count as passing, and saved that record. Then we ran it once. Every measure passed, and for — the strength matched what we had seen before (hollow dots in the chart above).

We checked the obvious explanations. Every score already excludes listening volume, exploring a lot, liking mainstream music, how long someone used Last.fm and data quality. Being part of a scene that grew is the biggest one: liking indie folk just before indie folk took off makes someone look prescient without them choosing well within it. We count only picking well relative to others in the same scene, and measure scene-picking separately (the "scenes" rows above).

What this can't tell us

  • Influence versus foresight. Someone who shares music with friends partly causes the growth they get credit for. The data has no friendships, so we can't separate the two.
  • Who these listeners are. Last.fm users who were still active in 2012–13 lean towards indie and rock, and are keener than the average listener.
  • The era. This is mostly before algorithmic playlists. Whether taste works the same way when an algorithm suggests your next song is open.

What's next

Repeating the analysis on ListenBrainz (2015–2026) to see whether taste holds in the streaming-algorithm era, and looking at what predicts good taste. If it holds up, a site that scores someone's own listening history becomes possible, with an honest range rather than a single number.

Technical appendix

Definitions

  • Data: MLHD+ (Last.fm scrobbles, — listeners, — billion listens), split at random by listener into an explore half and a hidden (sealed) half.
  • Bet: first committed adoption (4 distinct listening weeks within 26 weeks) after a 26-week onboarding period, dated at commitment. Song bets come from the 1/8 of listeners with full listening detail; songs by one artist adopted in the same month count as one bet. Scene bets: entering a scene new to the listener.
  • Comparison: other listeners' bets in the same month and popularity group (fixed panel, the listener excluded), adjusted for scene, artist or song age, prior growth, country and release timing.
  • Headline: rank correlation between a listener's score up to a cutoff year and their score from bets made two or more years later, after removing what impostor traits (measured up to the cutoff) explain.
  • Confirmation (fixed before the hidden half was analysed): Holm-corrected one-sided test within each family (discernment; the other nine) at 5%, and 3-year stability above zero.
