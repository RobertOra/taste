Is taste real?

Early answer: partly yes. People whose picks outgrew comparable artists in 2006–08 tended to do it again in 2010–11 (correlation — across — users), after accounting for scene, artist age, momentum and country. About a third of the raw signal was simply being in scenes that grew.

Four ways to define taste

Persistence across separate periods for each model, before and after controls. A: your picks grow more than comparable artists. B: your taste community adopts them later. C: they're still listened to in 2026. D: other people who try them stick with them.

A (prescience) is the cleanest result: small influence from impostors, stable across every setting tested.

B (community-leading) and D (discernment) show the highest numbers after controls, but impostors explain more of them than of A (— and —). B may still pick up trend-following inside your own community, and D may partly reflect music types whose fans are loyal anyway. Treat both with caution.

C (durability) is weak once controls are applied: whether your picks are still big in 2026 mostly reflects how mainstream they were.

What the controls remove (model A)

Each control compares a bet only with bets on artists that share that trait. Scene does most of the work; age, momentum and country add little. The last bar crossed every control into tiny groups, which compared bets mostly with other people picking the same artist and erased the signal mechanically. It was replaced by applying controls side by side.

Does it depend on the settings?

Model A with all controls, changing one setting at a time. The vertical line is the main version. A flat cluster means the choice doesn't matter. Hollow dots ran on a random half or quarter of users and compare to the reference rows at the bottom. Hover a dot for more.

  • Almost every setting lands close to the main version, so the result doesn't hinge on any one choice.
  • Dating bets at the first listen instead of at commitment inflates the score, as expected: it credits trend-following during the weeks someone was deciding.
  • A yes/no "big hit" score shows almost nothing. Taste here looks like steady outperformance, not catching the occasional huge winner.

Do the models agree?

Correlation between people's scores on each pair of models (all controls). One shared factor explains — of the variation across the four scores, against one in four if they were unrelated. A and D are partly linked mechanically: an artist that keeps the people who try it also grows more.

Reading it

Prescience and discernment go together most. Durability pulls slightly the other way from discernment: artists whose fans are loyal tend to be niche, while artists still big in 2026 tend to be mainstream.

So far this looks less like one general taste ability and more like two or three related ones.

What this can't say yet

Open explanations

  • Following music news. Being early just before a big album release may mean reading blogs, not having taste. Needs release dates.
  • Influence. Someone who shares music with friends partly causes the growth they're credited with predicting. Friendships aren't in the data.
  • Who is in the data. Everyone here was still on Last.fm in 2012–13, and the user base skews indie.
  • Era. This is mostly before algorithmic playlists. ListenBrainz (2015–2026) is the comparison.

Why popularity is measured within a fixed panel

Active users in the explore half grew many times over the period, so raw listener counts make nearly every artist look like it's growing.
